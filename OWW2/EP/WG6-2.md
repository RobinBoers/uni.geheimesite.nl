1. The paper’s main claim(s)
minimal guidance is less effective, but PBL is not always minimal guidance, it is a method in which the amount of guidance is flexible. PBL is in fact compatible with human cognitive architecture, and maybe even more so than direct instruction.

2. The evidence that supports the claim(s)

discussion activates prior knowledge:
- Schmidt et al. (1989), novices recalled 40% more after discussing a problem, experts 11% more (hints at expertise-reversal effect)
- Schmidt (1993), health science students recalled almost 2x as much after discussing a problem

support/scaffolding is needed and available:
- Schmidt (1994), the expertise of the tutor is more important if prior knowledge is low, or instruction unstructured (consistent with CLT)

PBL leads to better application:
- Dochy et al. (2003) meta-analysis

3. How it responds to the arguments by Kirschner, Sweller and Clark

- it shows that KSC misunderstand how PBL works: PBL has the goal "scaffolding for student independence". KSC confuse this goal (for experts) with the method used (for novices).
- it shows that PBL takes into account CLT, because PBL elements exist to manage cognitive load:
  - seven-step training + restricted resources -> extreanous load
  - simple-to-complex order + tutor assistence + distributing load among group -> intrinsic load
  - discussions + tutor prompts -> germane load
- it addresses the argumentation & evidence used
  - complexity mentioned by KSC is CLT-complexity, not PBL-complexity, so the comparison is not entirely fair
  - evidence mentioned by KSC is for individual learning, while PBL is about group-based learning, so the comparison is not entirely fair
  - curriculum comparisons are not randomized + may show ceiling effects
  - pbl optimizes for different transfer (not solving specific new problems per se, but *how* to solve problems in general; you test direct instruction by giving new problems to solve, you test pbl by testing how well you learn next time), so again, comparison might not be entirelt fair

4. What does it challenge or leave unresolved?

- it challenges the categorization, not the main claim, because they agree with the claim, but just think it does not apply to PBL
- the argument about implementation is not strong, because PBL can be badly implemented, and badly implemented PBL *is* minimal guidance, and KSC critisize the PBL they see in practise
- authentic problems can have high intrinsic load, even for the "simple" problems (especially because "simple" problems are *PBL-simple*, not necessarily *CLT-simple*!!)
- the claim "works better than direct instruction" is not entirely supported, and there is no experimental evidence that compares PBL and worked examples (because the study discussed compares problem discussion to discussion of unrelated problem, not to worked example)
- the learning still happens individually, from the provided resources, which is, if you think about it, actually just direct instruction. so they essentially argue for direct instruction wrapped in authentic problems. i wonder what the effectiveness of PBL is on its own, if you leave out the direct instruction part.

Presenting
----------

1. Share your paper’s main claim, its strongest support, and how it responds to Kirschner, Sweller and Clark

Our paper's main claim is that problem-based learning is not (always) minimally guided, but that it is a form of instruction that is flexible in the amount of guidance, and that it is in fact compatible with human cognitive architecture, and maybe even more so than direct instruction is. It agrees with KSC that minimal guidance is an ineffective instructional method, but disagrees with the categorisation of PBL as minimally guided. It shows that PBL takes cognitive load into account with various elements. It uses 7 clearly structured steps and a restricted list of resources (so the learner doesn't have to search), which both reduce extraneous load. It orders problems from simple to complex, the cognitive load is distributed over the group, and there is a tutor to assist in case a group gets stuck. These all limit intrinsic load to a managable level (because it is true that the authentic problems used in PBL problems have a baseline higher instrinsic load than similar direct instruction approaches would have). And the group discussion and tutor prompting can both contribute to a higher germane load, and thus more learning. The empirical evidence shows that the group discussions in PBL lead to activation of prior knowledge, which for novices leads to a 40% higher recall rate, and for experts 11% higher, which hints at the expertise-reversal effect (but both are still higher!).

Designing
---------

1. Check (mis)conceptions of seasons by asking something like "why is it warmer in summer?", students all write it down and then a few explain.
2. Let them investigate, but have some scaffolding as to how, like:
- shine flashlight on paper at 2 angles, compare how big the spot is
- have the globe with the tilt, rotate it around lamp, and mark europe and australia (make sure tilt direction stays fixed!!)
3. Teacher can prompt for something like "if distance causes summer, what season should it be in australia in july?" and "where is the most light on the globe?"
4. Students revise their initial explanation, and maybe also explain it to a peer or something.

Checking design
---------------

1. Use your assigned perspective to review your lesson. Discuss what your perspective reveals. What change would improve the lesson?

- Motivation: Does the lesson support students’ competence, autonomy, or sense of connection?

1. Competence: in the original task, students got stuck => failure experience => lower self-efficacy. with cleared structure, they would have higher self-efficacy & competence, so more motivation.
2. Autonomy: structured steps do reduce autonomy, so from this viewpoint motivation would be less.
3. Relatedness: working in groups satisfies relatedness. Maybe choosing the groups themselves would be benificial too?

- Metacognition: How will students notice whether they understand?

in the original lesson, students had no indication whether they understood or not, and maybe using materials felt like false productivity / illusion of learning. there is no pre- or post-test.

writing down an explanation/prediction at the start and changing at the end if its is wrong can show that they now understand.

- Self-regulation: How will students plan and manage their work?

structured steps are given, so planning is less of a problem, I think, because the teacher regulates. you can solve that by giving them the steps and making them determine the order themselves. but the goal of the lesson isn't really self-regulation, is it? also, maybe some kind of support in telling them how to work together would be in order too.

- Cognitive architecture: Where might students get stuck or overloaded? What support could help?

Orbit, rotation, tilt, angle, energy and day length are all interacting elements, so there might be a high intrinsic load for beginners.

understanding separate elements in separate steps first, can maybe reduce intrinsic load a little.

and marking things physically on the globe can also reduce/offload the cognitive load partly.

---

So the problem is basically these perspectives "pulling" towards two sides:

- from CLT perspective, we need more structure
- from motivational perspective (and also a little self-regulation), we need more freedom/autonomy

the "solution" is probably the ordering/sequencing: first more structure and later on more autonomy (which is basically what schmidt et al. called "scaffolding for independence" btw.)
